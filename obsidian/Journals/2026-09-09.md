# Daily Journal: 2026-09-09

## A crossroads: what the alignment congruence work actually implies

After three cross-model pairings (XALIGNQ/TinyLlama, XALIGNDS/DeepSeek-R1-Distill,
XALIGNPHI/Phi-4-mini - see [[cross_model_alignment_congruence]],
[[cross_model_alignment_congruence_deepseek]],
[[cross_model_alignment_congruence_phi4]]) and 337 clean trials on the
strongest pairing (Phi-4-mini, 0.946 cosine similarity at
calibration_size=280, within 0.017 of same-model ALIGNQ's own 0.963),
today's discussion worked out what this actually means for the larger
goal - a shared representational "language" between models - and where
to go next.

### Discovered, not created

The key fact: Phi-4-mini-instruct was never co-trained with Qwen, never
exposed to any shared protocol. A single orthogonal rotation, calibrated
from a few hundred example concepts, still reaches 0.95 cosine
similarity. A rotation is a restricted kind of map - it can only recover
a correspondence that already existed in both spaces' geometry, it can't
invent one. If the two models' concept spaces were genuinely different
"languages" - arbitrary, independently-evolved symbol systems, the way
English and Chinese are - no rotation could bridge them, any more than
rotating English could produce Chinese.

That rotation works at all is direct evidence the two models were never
speaking different languages to begin with. They're closer to two
photographs of the same scene taken from different angles than two
different alphabets - the Platonic Representation Hypothesis
([[research-precedents]]) made concrete. The "Newspeak" idea raised in
conversation isn't something that needs inventing and then teaching to a
community through trial and error and repetition, the way human children
learn language. It's closer to a reference frame that already exists in
each model independently - calibration is the (cheap) act of finding the
rotation between two given frames, not teaching a new one. Neither
instant mutual recognition (an untranslated vector doesn't work) nor slow
social learning through repeated exposure - a third thing, more like
tuning two already-compatible radios to the same frequency.

### The gap this doesn't close: recognition vs. interpretation

Raised via a sharp analogy: hearing an unfamiliar human language, you
instantly recognize *that it is language* even though you can't decode
it - recognition and interpretation are different capabilities. Every
metric measured so far (congruence, cosine similarity, top-1 accuracy)
only tests whether a translated vector *geometrically lands near the
right target*. None of it tests whether a receiving model, given that
vector, actually *behaves* as if it understood it. That's the real gap
between "good alignment" and "working communication," and it's the
natural next experiment.

### Two concrete refinements to how that next experiment should be built

1. **Baseline against the old P3LOOP soft-prompt-injection attempt**
   before assuming today's much-improved rotation (validated calibration
   size, no centering, Phi-4-mini as the strongest target found) will
   fare any better. Expectation going in: probably still not close to
   precise, single-concept injection into an otherwise-unprompted
   generation is a weak test - but worth establishing where the baseline
   actually sits now rather than assuming.

2. **Test with a sequence of related concepts, not a single injected
   token.** LLMs are trained to continue/complete a string of context,
   not to echo back one isolated embedding - a single dangling injected
   vector with no surrounding structure is out-of-distribution for what
   the model ever saw during training, which is likely a real part of why
   P3LOOP's original outputs were incoherent. Translating several related
   concepts from the same domain (physics terms, say) into the target
   model's space and feeding them as a short soft-prompt sequence is both
   a more natural fit to how these models actually operate and a more
   forgiving, more meaningful signal to evaluate ("did the continuation
   land in the right domain/theme") than checking for an exact token
   match ever was. A three-way comparison - rotated sequence vs.
   unrotated/random sequence (negative control) vs. the real-text
   equivalent prompt (upper-bound reference) - would make whatever comes
   out of this properly interpretable rather than anecdotal.

### Where this leaves the bigger picture

What's been built so far is pairwise - one calibrated rotation between
two specific models. It doesn't generalize to a third model without a
new calibration. vec2vec's "universal latent space"
([[research-precedents]]) is the natural infrastructure answer if a
genuine shared auxiliary language (N calibrations instead of N² pairwise
ones) turns out to be worth building - but that's premature before
knowing whether pairwise translation produces anything a receiving model
can actually *use*, not just anything that measures well geometrically.
Order of operations settled on: interpretation test first, universal
space later if warranted.

## Follow-up: the first interpretation test, and a real design correction

The three-condition version described above (isolated concept vectors,
injected via `generate(inputs_embeds=...)`) was built and run on real
hardware. Real text produced clean, on-topic physics continuations,
confirming the harness itself was sound - but both the rotated and
random-rotation conditions collapsed into degenerate repetition loops
("r I I on the r I I on the...", "de la media de la media..."), and
critically, the calibrated rotation did no better than the random one.
That made it impossible to tell "the translation carries no usable
meaning" apart from "the injection method itself breaks the model,"
since both failed identically.

Working through why with the user surfaced a real methodological bug,
not evidence against the underlying alignment work:
**`inputs_embeds` only ever supplies a model's *input* layer** (before
any transformer blocks run), but the validated rotation maps onto
Phi-4-mini's **layer 24** hidden state - already 24 layers deep into
processing. Feeding that in as `inputs_embeds` forces a mid-stack
snapshot through all 32 layers as if it were raw input text, a category
mismatch severe enough to explain the collapse on its own, independent of
translation quality.

Two corrections followed, both prompted by the user pushing on the
design rather than accepting the first explanation at face value:

1. **Inject at the model's own matching layer via a forward hook**, not
   via `inputs_embeds`. The hook overrides the residual stream at layer
   24 only for the initial prefill pass; every token generated afterward
   runs layers 25-32 normally, same as any other input would.
2. **Translate the full token sequence of a real passage, not four
   isolated concept summaries.** The concept-level reduction was
   inherited from the alignment-*measurement* work (ALIGNQ etc.), where
   it's genuinely necessary - you need discrete, nameable units to check
   against ground truth. It was carried into the *generation* test
   without re-examining whether that necessity actually applied there,
   and it doesn't: a fixed rotation is just a linear map, applicable to
   any vector in the right space, whether that vector is a last-token
   concept summary or token 47 of an ordinary paragraph. Full-sequence
   translation is not meaningfully more expensive on this hardware (same
   forward pass either way, just reading out more of it) and is both a
   more honest test of what a real translator needs to do and a fix for
   part of the original collapse - isolated vectors have no relational
   structure between them the way real passage positions do.

A fourth condition was added on the same reasoning: **self round-trip** -
capture the target model's own native layer-24 hidden state for the same
passage (no source model, no translation at all) and re-inject it through
the identical hook mechanism. This isolates whether the injection
mechanism itself is lossy independent of cross-model translation quality.
Prediction going in: probably not fully transparent (the captured vector
loses whatever surrounding sentence context it originally had, now
replaced by neutral placeholder positions), but meaningfully more
coherent than the cross-model conditions, since it removes a distinct
candidate failure mode - a Procrustes rotation preserves vector
*direction* but not necessarily *scale*, and two independently-trained
models plausibly have systematically different activation magnitudes at
a given layer, which could push the network into an unfamiliar regime
independent of whether the rotation's direction is meaningful at all.

Rebuilt (`src/piper_geometry/concept_transfer_test.py`) and unit-tested
against synthetic fake models before running on real hardware - the
layer-injection hook, the full-sequence extraction, and the
output-slicing (excluding the placeholder prefix from decoded output)
were each verified independently. Ready to run for real next.

## Follow-up: real hardware results, a scale-mismatch fix, and a 10-trial batch

The redesigned four-condition test ran for real. Confirmed directly with
numbers: Qwen's layer-18 vectors average norm ~79, Phi-4-mini's own
native layer-24 vectors average ~291 - about 3.7x larger. A Procrustes
rotation is provably norm-preserving, so translated vectors carried
Qwen's scale unchanged, landing far outside what Phi-4-mini's later
layers were ever trained to process. Rescaling by the mean-norm ratio
fixed some of the resulting numerical instability but not the underlying
coherence problem.

A 10-trial batch (`src/piper_geometry/ctransfer_batch.py`, subprocess-per-trial
so a CUDA device-side assert in one trial can't corrupt the process for
the rest) corrected a conclusion drawn too early from n=2: random rotation
crashed 4/10 times, not consistently - not the clean "calibrated survives,
random always crashes" story the first two runs suggested. More
important finding from the 6 successful trials: **self round-trip -
Phi-4-mini's own real, zero-translation-error activations, re-injected
through the same mechanism - never stayed on-topic either**, drifting into
unrelated narratives even with perfect input fidelity. That reframes the
bottleneck: it's not (only) translation quality, it's that isolated
injected snapshots lose whatever context originally produced them,
regardless of how faithfully the vector itself is reproduced.

## A bigger reframing: what "communication" should mean here, and a concrete path to a trained model

A wide-ranging discussion connected several things into one practical
conclusion.

**Weights don't transfer, even between identically-trained models** - not
just due to random initialization, but because of permutation symmetry:
swapping two neurons in a hidden layer (and their associated weights)
computes the exact same function, so there's no canonical labeling of
"the same neuron" across two training runs. This is *why* the whole
field (and this project) works on activations/representations rather
than raw weights - representations are far less sensitive to this
internal relabeling freedom than weights are.

**The qualia/subjective-experience question (the "orange" problem) turns
out to be the philosophical justification for that same choice.** Two
people don't need matching subjective experience of orange to
communicate about it - they need matching *relational structure* (orange
sits between red and yellow, closer to red than to blue). That
relational geometry is public and comparable even if any private
experience riding along with it never is. The rotation/cosine-similarity
work has been measuring exactly that relational structure, never
anything about "experience" - which means it doesn't need to resolve
whether a model understands anything to be meaningful, only whether the
structure is functionally isomorphic.

**JEPA** (Yann LeCun's research program) makes a related argument from a
different angle: predicting raw tokens/pixels wastes capacity on
unpredictable surface detail, while predicting in a learned embedding
space captures the actually-predictable abstract structure. Its own
method compares embeddings, not raw output - the same shift already
agreed on here (judge task/behavior correctness in representation space,
not generation fluency).

**The evolutionary framing turned out to be a precise diagnosis, not a
metaphor.** What makes evolution directional rather than merely noisy is
variation + selection + retention together. Temperature-sampled
generation has variation but no selection or retention - every attempt
is independent and discarded regardless of quality, nothing compounds.
Gradient-descent training *is* a variation-selection-retention process
(a step explores a direction, the loss keeps what helps and discards
what doesn't, the update persists into the next step) - which is exactly
why "inject more randomness" was never going to be the fix, and why a
trained adapter is the concrete shape of what was actually being asked
for.

### The concrete next build: a trained adapter, task-based loss, both base models frozen

Qwen and Phi-4-mini stay completely frozen throughout - only a new small
bridging module gets trained, keeping the actual research question
("can two independently-trained, unmodified models be bridged?") intact
rather than quietly becoming "can one model be retrained to accommodate
the other."

- **Adapter**: replaces the fixed closed-form rotation W (plus the
  hand-derived scale factor) with one trainable linear layer `A`
  (896x3072) + bias `b`, warm-started from the existing W rather than
  from scratch.
- **Downstream task**: a closed-set classification probe - inject a
  passage's translated hidden states, append a fixed real-text suffix
  ("This concept belongs to the field of"), read Phi-4-mini's next-token
  logits at the final position, cross-entropy loss against the true
  domain label. Concrete, checkable, differentiable - a real answer to
  "how do we measure downstream behavior," not generation fluency.
- **Training loop**: sample a domain + passage, extract Qwen's hidden
  states (no grad - Qwen's weights aren't being trained), apply the
  adapter (grad required here), inject via the same layer-24 hook (one
  forward pass, not full generate()), compute the loss, backprop through
  Phi-4-mini's frozen-but-differentiable layers into just `A`/`b`,
  optimizer step.
- **Evaluation**: held-out domains/passages never trained on, tracked
  over training steps, to check generalization rather than
  memorization - plus periodic qualitative re-checks via
  `concept_transfer_test.py`'s free-generation comparison, to see whether
  a classification-trained adapter also improves coherence as a side
  effect even though that was never the direct target.

Not yet built - this is the design, next session's task is the
implementation.

### Build complete: `train_adapter.py`

Implemented in `src/piper_geometry/train_adapter.py`, in two commits
(`9f6c731` scaffold, `ea05f2e` training loop):

- `TranslationAdapter` - the trainable `nn.Linear`, warm-started from
  `build_rotation`'s closed-form W times a measured scale factor.
- `load_examples` - splits `concepts_dictionary.json` into per-domain
  train/held-out sets (seeded, so re-runs start from the same boundary).
- `resolve_label_token_ids` - maps each domain's label word to Phi-4-mini's
  actual first-subword token id, for the cross-entropy target.
- `forward_with_injection` - the differentiable counterpart to
  `generate_with_injection`: one forward pass (not `generate()`), reading
  logits at the position right after the suffix. Needed a new
  `PartialLayerInjectionHook` (in `layer_injection.py`) since the injected
  content is now only a *leading span* of a longer sequence that also
  carries the suffix's real tokens - the original hook assumed its whole
  input was the injected span.
- `compute_loss_and_prediction` / `evaluate` / `save_checkpoint` /
  `run_training` / `train` - the loop itself, held-out accuracy, and
  checkpointing (saved only on improvement), with `run_training` factored
  apart from `train()` so the loop logic is testable without a real model
  download.

Verified locally with a synthetic two-domain toy task (fully
differentiable fake target model standing in for Phi-4-mini): loss drops
0.87 -> 0.01 and held-out accuracy reaches 1.0 over 150 steps, confirming
gradient genuinely reaches the adapter through the injection hook. Real
run against Qwen/Phi-4-mini is next, on the Jetson:

```
python3 src/piper_geometry/train_adapter.py --num-steps 200
```

### First real Jetson run: two bugs, then a real result

First attempt (200 steps) diverged to nan loss by step 4 and never
recovered - printed "Scale factor: 1.000x" instead of the expected
~3.7-3.9x. Root cause: `compute_scale_factor` was calling
`ResidualExtractor.extract_activations()`, which L2-normalizes every
vector it returns to unit length (an existing property of that method,
not something added for this) - so the ratio it measured was always
~1.0 regardless of the real magnitude gap between Qwen and Phi-4-mini's
native activation scales. Combined with computing cross-entropy directly
on Phi-4-mini's native fp16 logits (a known `log_softmax` overflow
source), loss corrupted to nan and stayed there - and because
checkpointing only fired on an accuracy improvement, nothing was ever
saved from that run to even diagnose.

Fixed (commit `19641fd`): `compute_scale_factor` now uses
`extract_full_sequence`'s raw, unnormalized hidden states; the loss casts
logits to float32 before `cross_entropy`; a non-finite loss now skips
that step's optimizer update instead of poisoning Adam's moment
estimates permanently; gradient norms are clipped as an extra margin;
and a checkpoint always saves on the first eval regardless of accuracy,
so a derailed run still leaves something to inspect.

Second run (200 steps, same command): scale factor measured 3.509x - in
line with the earlier passage-based estimate. Held-out accuracy climbed
0.017 -> 0.217 -> 0.450 -> 0.633 -> 0.650 -> **0.817** (step 150, well
above the ~16.7% random-guess baseline for 6 domains), then declined to
0.717 by step 200 - the checkpoint-on-improvement logic correctly kept
the step-150 checkpoint rather than the worse final state. 12 scattered
nan/inf steps occurred across the run (mostly in the back half) and were
all cleanly skipped by the new guard without derailing training.

**Saved checkpoint** (`data/checkpoints/trained_adapter.pt`, copied
Jetson -> Quantum PC): step=150, held_out_accuracy=0.8167,
source_layer=18, receiver_layer=24. `linear.weight` shape (3072, 896),
norm 105.09; `linear.bias` norm 0.198 - both clearly moved from their
warm-started rotation/zero-bias starting point, confirming real gradient
updates happened.

This is the first concrete evidence that task-based training can push
the adapter meaningfully beyond the fixed closed-form rotation's
generalization on this classification proxy. Open questions for next
steps: what's driving the late-training decline (175-200) and the
scattered nan spikes even after the float32 fix - possibly the learning
rate is a bit high for this regime, or a specific domain/label is
ill-conditioned; worth a per-domain accuracy breakdown and/or a learning
rate sweep before trusting 0.817 as a stable number rather than a lucky
peak.
